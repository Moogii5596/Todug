## Key question 1: Which specific parent strategies have the strongest evidence for expressive vocabulary and word combinations?

### Takeaway
The best-supported package is not a single branded programme but a cluster of generic techniques: responsive, child-led interaction (follow the lead, wait, take balanced turns), high-frequency modelling of a small set of target words in play and routines (focused stimulation), and expanding the child's utterance by one step (expansion/recast), plus dialogic book reading. Meta-analyses put the average effect of parent-implemented intervention on child expressive language/vocabulary in the small-to-medium range (g roughly 0.3-0.5), with larger effects for children with primary language delay than for at-risk or secondary-impairment groups, and with the clearest effect being on the parent's own behaviour.

### Cited Findings

**Meta-analyses of parent-implemented language intervention**
- Roberts & Kaiser (2011), *AJSLP* 20:180-199: 18 studies; effect sizes computed for 7 language outcomes with a random-effects model; conclusion that parent-implemented interventions have a significant positive effect on receptive and expressive language in children with and without intellectual disability — [ASHA / AJSLP](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2011/10-0055))
- Same review, by comparison type (as reported by a later secondary summary; not checked against the original): vs no-treatment/business-as-usual, g = .35 to .81 with six of seven outcomes significant; vs therapist-implemented treatment, g = -.15 to .42 with only two significant, i.e. trained parents were roughly as effective as therapists — [PMC3740334](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3740334)
- Roberts, Curtis, Sone & Hampton (2019), *JAMA Pediatrics* 173(7):671-680: 76 studies (59 randomised, 17 non-randomised), 5,848 participants, mean child age 3.5 years, children with primary or secondary language impairment or at risk; parent training was positively associated with child language and communication — [JAMA Pediatrics](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2733860)
- Same review: parent use of language support strategies rose with Hedges g = 0.55; overall child-outcome effect reported as 0.33 across expressive and receptive language, engagement and social skills — [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/348af982-06b3-4189-984e-125758f85e15)
- Same review: authors call for research on which specific components of parent training drive parent strategy use (i.e. the active ingredients are not yet isolated) — [JAMA Pediatrics](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2733860)
- Heidlage et al. (2020, online 2019), *Early Childhood Research Quarterly*: 25 RCTs published 1992-2015, children aged 0-8 — [PMC9911096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9911096)
- Heidlage et al.: g = 0.42 (medium) for expressive vocabulary, g = 0.18 for receptive vocabulary, g = 0.27 for expressive language per the ASHA Evidence Map abstract — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/2df79249-9828-4a65-9c01-ff8a849e7a39); a repository record attaches the 0.42 to "expressive language" and gives receptive language g = 0.07 (non-significant) — [ETSU record](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works/9145). CONFLICT in labels between sources; verify in the paper before quoting.
- Heidlage et al., by intervention context, expressive vocabulary: shared book reading g = 0.37; play- or routine-based intervention g = 0.50; both significant — [ETSU record](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works/9145)
- Heidlage et al.: effects larger for children with primary language impairment than secondary — [Northwestern EI lab](https://ei.northwestern.edu/?p=744)
- Cochrane review, Law, Garrett & Nye (2003), CD004110 (primary speech/language delay): expressive vocabulary SMD = 0.89 (95% CI 0.21-1.56); expressive syntax SMD = 1.02 (95% CI 0.04-2.01, mixed findings, n = 233); receptive SMD = -0.04 (little support); no significant difference between clinician-administered intervention and intervention implemented by trained parents — [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD004110)

**Late-talker-specific reviews**
- Cable & Domsch (2011), *IJLCD* 46:138-154, the first systematic review for late language emergence (children 24-36 months, studies 1985-2008): treatment improved formal language scores, MLU and target-word use with medium-to-large effects in good-quality studies; "focused stimulation and modelling of single words" can improve late talkers' language — [IJLCD](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.3109/13682822.2010.487883)
- DeVeney, Hagaman & Bjornsen (2017), *Communication Disorders Quarterly* 39(1):293-302: 8 studies / 7 data sets, N = 175; both parent- and clinician-delivered treatment help late talkers; parent-implemented emerged as "potentially more effective" — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/ee217ad2-a906-479e-9b91-2cd2efd60825)

**Focused stimulation / target words (Hanen Target Word lineage)**
- Girolametto, Pearce & Weitzman (1996): pilot with 16 mothers of children with expressive delay; treatment-group children used more target words than controls. An earlier general-stimulation version had weak results, which led to adding focused stimulation — [PMC8345947](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8345947)
- Current Target Word programme: the SLP helps the parent pick 10 target words the child will be motivated to use — [Hanen](https://hanen.org/programs/tw)
- VAULT (Alt et al. 2020, *JSLHR*), a clinician-delivered focused-stimulation protocol for late talkers using varied sentence frames and contexts: beat no treatment with an average effect size near 1.0 — [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2019_JSLHR-19-00219)

**Enhanced Milieu Teaching (EMT)**
- Core EMT components: environmental arrangement, responsive interaction, modelling target-level language, expansions, time delays, milieu prompting — [Kaiser & Hampton chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265037755_Enhanced_Milieu_Teaching)
- Roberts & Kaiser (2015), *Pediatrics* 135(4):686-693: RCT of caregiver-delivered EMT, 97 toddlers aged 24-42 months scoring at least 1.33 SD below the mean on Bayley language; EMT group improved in receptive language (PLS-4) immediately post-intervention; What Works Clearinghouse rates it as meeting standards without reservations — [PMC4379460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4379460); [WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/90574)
- Follow-up at 6 and 12 months: Hampton, Kaiser & Roberts (2017), *Pediatrics* — [Northwestern](https://ei.northwestern.edu/one-year-language-outcomes-in-toddlers-with-language-delays-an-rct-follow-up)
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of EMT exists (ASHA journals) — [ASHA](https://on.asha.org/emt-review) (contents not retrieved)

**Heidelberg Parent-based Language Intervention (HPLI)**
- Buschmann et al. (2009), *Arch Dis Child*: RCT, 58 two-year-olds with specific expressive language delay, 29 intervention vs 29 twelve-month waiting list; mothers took the 3-month HPLI; final analysed sample 47. At follow-up 8.3% of intervention children vs 26.1% of waiting-list children met criteria for specific language impairment — [PMC2614563](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2614563/)
- Longer follow-up (Buschmann et al. 2015, *J Early Intervention*, n = 23 vs 20): nine months after intervention the groups differed significantly in active vocabulary and grammar in favour of intervention — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1053815115609384)

**Dialogic / shared book reading**
- Mol, Bus, de Jong & Smeets (2008), *Early Education and Development* 19:7-26: 16 studies of dialogic vs ordinary parent-child reading; expressive vocabulary d = .59 (k = 9, n = 322; 95% CI .44-.75) — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2899524/view)
- Same meta-analysis as cited secondarily: effect much smaller for at-risk children (d = 0.13) than not-at-risk (d = 0.53), and smaller for older (4-5) than younger (2-3) children — [UWO critical review](https://www.uwo.ca/fhs/csd/ebp/reviews/2012-13/Atkinson_A.pdf) (secondary; not verified against original)
- A 2020 *JSLHR* meta-analysis of shared book reading exists (Liverpool group) — [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2020_JSLHR-19-00288?download=true) (results not retrieved)

**Word combinations / early sentences: "toy talk" (Hadley & Rispoli)**
- Toy talk is a parent strategy with two parts: talk about the toys, and give the object its name, so that parents produce sentences with noun (lexical NP) subjects ("The juice is gone") instead of pronoun-heavy input ("That's a bottle", "You're feeding the baby") — [Univ. of Illinois](https://www.ahs.illinois.edu/?p=2923)
- Hadley et al. (2017), *JSLHR*: 19 treatment parents; instruction substantially increased parents' lexical NP subject tokens and types versus controls; parents' NP-subject *types* predicted growth in children's sentence diversity, although treatment condition by itself did not — [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-L-17-0008)
- "Sentence diversity" = number of unique subject + verb combinations in the child's sentences; lexical NP subject diversity and full "is" declaratives in input were predictors in follow-up work — [J Child Language](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C3E58274C1B7B2B968D5DB9D88737C58/S0305000922000459a.pdf/parent-responsivity-language-input-and-the-development-of-simple-sentences.pdf)

**Conversational turns (LENA / 3Ts)**
- Gilkerson et al. longitudinal work: conversational turn counts at 18-24 months explained 14-27% of variance in IQ, verbal comprehension and vocabulary ten years later, controlling for SES (correlational) — [PMC6192025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6192025)
- LENA Start preliminary evaluation (*J Child Language*): non-equivalent groups, 39 LENA Start dyads vs 17 generic parent-education dyads; small-to-medium effects favouring LENA Start, significant for child vocalisations and conversational turns, not for adult word count; 72% graduated — [Cambridge](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/abs/parent-education-to-improve-early-language-development-a-preliminary-evaluation-of-lena-starttm/9ABEDE26C14B4677DC8D2E2373EEB27A)
- TMW/3Ts (Suskind et al. 2016 pilot): adult words, conversational turns and child vocalisations rose significantly *during* the intervention but not post-intervention — [TMW Center](https://tmwcenter.uchicago.edu/resources/enriching-home-language-environment-among-families-from-low-ses-backgrounds)
- 3Ts home visiting RCT (92 low-SES dyads, 46 per arm, curriculum from 14 to 20 months): intervention raised parental knowledge at 26 months, which led to more conversational turns at 38 months and better preschool language (mediation analysis) — [PMC10835758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10835758)

**Joint attention / autism-oriented parent coaching (stage 1 of the Todug ladder)**
- JASPER, caregiver-mediated (Kasari, Gulsrud, Paparella, Hellemann & Berry 2015): toddlers randomised to 10 weeks of hands-on parent coaching vs parent-only psychoeducation; significant effect on the primary outcome of joint engagement, plus play diversity and level, generalising to the classroom — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/33h1z7ks)
- Mechanism: the parent strategy "mirrored pacing" mediated the effect on joint engagement; joint engagement mediated 69% of the effect on initiating joint attention — [PMC8320675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8320675)
- P-ESDM (Rogers et al. 2012, *JAACAP* 51(10):1052-65): RCT, 98 toddlers aged 14-24 months at risk for ASD, 12 weeks at 1 hour/week of therapist contact; NO effect of group on parent-child interaction or on any child outcome — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23021480/)
- Rogers et al. (2019) note that RCTs of parent-implemented autism interventions generally improve parent skills but have not generally shown child-outcome effects — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-018-3740-5)

**Hanen It Takes Two to Talk (ITTT)**
- Parents learn child-oriented, interaction-promoting and language-modelling (responsive interaction) strategies; early efficacy studies cited by Hanen are Girolametto (1988) and Tannock, Girolametto & Siegel (1992) — [Hanen research summary](https://hanen.org/about-us/who-we-are-research/it-takes-two-to-talk-research-summary) (source is the programme owner; promotional)
- "Observe, Wait and Listen (OWL)" is described by Hanen as a core strategy for everyday moments; the "Four S's" are Say less, Stress, Go slow, Show, paired with "Repeat, repeat, repeat" — [Hanen handout](https://www.hanen.org/getmedia/18f8fd9d-8ef7-41d7-818f-5bb165dde4e3/AM-Handout-2026.pdf?ext=.pdf)

### Inferences
- Ranking by strength of evidence for *expressive vocabulary* in late talkers: (1) focused stimulation of a small target-word set inside responsive play/routines (Cable & Domsch; Girolametto 1996; VAULT; HPLI), (2) EMT-style expansions + time delay within responsive interaction (Roberts & Kaiser 2015), (3) dialogic reading (Mol 2008; Heidlage book-reading subgroup g = 0.37), with play/routine-based delivery slightly ahead of book-reading-only (g = 0.50 vs 0.37).
- For *word combinations*, direct parent-implemented RCT evidence is thin. The usable techniques are expansion by one element (EMT/Hanen), and toy talk's noun-subject sentence modelling, whose evidence is that it reliably changes parent input and that the input feature predicts child sentence diversity, not that the treatment arm itself beat control.
- The most robust finding across all meta-analyses is that parents change (g about 0.55); child effects are smaller and less consistent. Lesson design should therefore optimise for the parent actually doing the behaviour, not for content volume.
- Turn-count programmes (LENA Start, 3Ts) are universal/at-risk prevention with mostly correlational or mediated evidence; they support a "balanced turns" lesson principle but are weaker evidence than the late-talker trials.
- UNVERIFIED background (not confirmed this session; verify before publishing): PEER = Prompt, Evaluate, Expand, Repeat; CROWD = Completion, Recall, Open-ended, Wh-, Distancing prompts (Whitehurst). EMT's Teach-Model-Coach-Review is the caregiver-coaching cycle used by Kaiser/Roberts. Hanen's four communication stages are Discoverer, Communicator, First Words User, Combiner. Parallel talk = narrating what the child is doing; self-talk = narrating what the adult is doing. "Communicative temptations" originate with Wetherby & Prizant (e.g. giving a closed container, a wind-up toy that stops) as a way to create a reason to communicate.

### Gaps
- No full texts were readable, so exact Roberts & Kaiser 2015 effect sizes (expressive vocabulary, number of different words) and parent-fidelity numbers (matched turns, expansions, time delays) could not be extracted. I recall the trial favoured EMT on vocabulary measures as well as receptive language but could not confirm.
- Roberts et al. 2019 subgroup effects (ASD vs DLD vs at-risk) and moderators were not retrievable.
- No source retrieved isolating the effect of parallel talk / self-talk on their own; they appear only as components of packages.
- No source retrieved for UK-style Parent-Child Interaction therapy for language (e.g. Falkus et al. 2016), More Than Words trials (e.g. Carter et al. 2011), Project ImPACT trials, Vroom, Talk With Me Baby, or Harvard "serve and return". Reach Out and Read is covered under question 4/2 only from secondary raters.
- First-verbs lists (Hadley, Rispoli & Hsu 2016) and Lahey/Brown stage content were searched but not retrieved.
- Evidence is overwhelmingly from English and German; nothing found on Mongolian (an agglutinative, verb-final language, where "verbs" and "two-word combination" milestones may not map one-to-one).

## Key question 2: What dosage and structure do effective programmes use?

### Takeaway
Effective programmes run about 10-13 weeks (range 6 weeks to 6 months), deliver roughly weekly coached contact, and nearly always include live or video feedback on the parent's own interaction; the one large trial of a short, low-intensity, group-only version with slow-to-talk toddlers (6 sessions) found no benefit. Within sessions, word-learning studies use very high repetition (dozens of models per target word per session) on a small set (about 3-10 words), and the 3-vs-6 word comparison found no difference.

### Cited Findings

**Programme structure**
- Hanen ITTT: orientation meeting, pre-programme assessment of each child, eight group training sessions and three individual video-feedback sessions, led by a Hanen-certified speech-language therapist; video sessions are scheduled between group sessions — [UCL review](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/educational-psychology/resources/CS1Bird22-25.pdf)
- Hanen says the video-feedback sessions (parent-child play recorded, parent coached during it, then reviewed together) are central to changing parent interaction — [Hanen ITTT](https://hanen.org/programs/ittt)
- Hanen Target Word: SLP and parent select 10 target words per child — [Hanen](https://hanen.org/programs/tw)
- A Target-Word-type adapted programme coded in the Cochrane review tables: 11 weeks, 150 minutes per week, 12 children, target-word probes at post-test — [Cochrane table, PMC8407295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8407295/table/CD004110-tblf-0016)
- EMT (Roberts & Kaiser 2015): parents received 28 intervention sessions teaching language support strategies; children assessed at baseline and 4, 10 and 16 months; comparison group got community speech-language services (per later trial protocol describing the study; sample given there as 88, vs 97 enrolled in the primary report) — [ClinicalTrials.gov protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/93/NCT03782493/Prot_SAP_001.pdf); [PMC4379460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4379460)
- EMT is delivered in routine, everyday activities — [PMC4379460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4379460)
- HPLI: 3-month group programme for mothers of two-year-olds — [PMC2614563](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2614563/)
- JASPER caregiver-mediated: 10 weeks of hands-on coaching — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/33h1z7ks)
- P-ESDM (2012): 12 weeks, 1 hour/week therapist contact, and no effect at that dose — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23021480/)
- Rogers et al. (2019) tested an enhanced P-ESDM adding motivational interviewing, multimodal learning tools and weekly home visits (45 children, 12-30 months) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-018-3740-5)
- 3Ts home visiting: curriculum delivered from child age 14 to 20 months — [PMC10835758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10835758)
- Let's Learn Language (Wake et al. 2011, *BMJ* 343:d4741): cluster RCT, toddlers at or below the 20th centile for expressive vocabulary at 18 months; modified Hanen "You Make the Difference" over six weekly group sessions vs usual care; at age 3 adjusted difference in expressive language -2.4 (95% CI -6.2 to 1.4, P = 0.21), receptive -0.3 (-4.2 to 3.7); "feasible and acceptable, but little evidence that it improved language or behaviour" — [BMJ](https://www.doi.org/10.1136/BMJ.D4741)

**Dose within sessions (models per word, number of target words)**
- VAULT (Alt et al. 2020): a "dose" = one clinician production of a target word; 270 doses per session in total; compared 3 target words at 90 doses each vs 6 target words at 45 doses each; 16 sessions; 24 late talkers; no difference between dose conditions, so "clinicians have some flexibility in number of words targeted and dose number" — [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2019_JSLHR-19-00219); [PMC7213481](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7213481)
- Alt et al. (2014) pilot of the cross-situational approach: 4 late-talking toddlers, 7-10 weeks, twice-weekly sessions — [PMC4476298](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4476298)
- A later parent-delivered cross-situational word-learning study used a high dose (at least 64 productions per target word) and concluded the precise dose needed to trigger word learning "remains unclear" — [IJSLP 2024](https://www.informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17549507.2024.2311931)
- Speech-sound (phonological) intervention intensity recommendation from a systematic review: 2-3 sessions weekly of 30-60 minutes with a minimum of 50-100 trials per session (clinician-delivered context) — [PMC11606383](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11606383/)

**Parent-coaching method**
- Behaviour-change-technique (BCT) analysis of parent-led language intervention (applying BCT Taxonomy v1) distinguishes Level 1 techniques (SLT changing the parent's interaction) from Level 2 (parent changing the child's communication); of ten Level 2 techniques, five were already in BCTTv1 and five were new SLT-specific ones — [IJLCD record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/cmedm,38062889)
- Foundational BCT-in-SLT papers: Atkinson & Stringer (2016), Spalding & Stringer (2016); the ABC-PALS trial pairs the taxonomy with Video Enhanced Observation on the hypothesis that the self-reflection video requires makes parents adopt techniques faster (hypothesis from a protocol, results not found) — [ISRCTN protocol](https://www.isrctn.com/editorial/retrieveFile/e5469c2b-56b3-426c-8180-3b0019005983/38035)
- Heidlage et al. note that poor reporting of parent-training procedures prevented analysis of *how* child outcomes were achieved — [ETSU record](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works/9145)
- A 2026 *AJSLP* systematic review examines the specific roles parents assume in these interventions — [AJSLP](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2026_AJSLP-25-00222) (contents not retrieved)

### Inferences
- A 4-week plan is shorter than every effective programme found (shortest effective: 10 weeks JASPER, about 12 weeks HPLI/ITTT; the 6-session LLL trial was null). Todug's 4-week block is best framed as one cycle of a repeating plan (three cycles approximates the 12-week norm), not as a complete intervention.
- Because the 3-words/90-doses vs 6-words/45-doses comparison was null, lesson design can pick 3-6 focus words per week (Hanen uses 10 over the programme) and aim for many natural repetitions per word per play session rather than a precise count. The honest statement to parents is "many repetitions across varied situations", not a magic number.
- The components that separate effective from null trials appear to be individual feedback on the parent's own interaction (video or live coaching) and sufficient duration; a self-guided web product lacks the coach, so self-video, self-rating checklists, a single weekly goal and demonstration videos are the nearest substitutes. This is an inference: no study found tested a fully self-guided digital version against a coached one.
- UNVERIFIED background: Target Word is commonly described as 5 group sessions plus 2 individual video consultations for children roughly 18-30 months; HPLI as seven 2-hour sessions over 3 months plus a booster about 6 months later, groups of 5-10 parents; LENA Start as 10-13 weekly one-hour group sessions with a weekly daylong recording and feedback report; Roberts & Kaiser's EMT sessions as about 1 hour, clinic plus home, over roughly 3 months, each following Teach-Model-Coach-Review. More Than Words: 8 group sessions plus 3 video visits for parents of autistic children under about 5. Check each before citing.
- UNVERIFIED background on minutes per day: programmes generally ask parents to embed strategies in existing routines (meals, bath, dressing, play, books) rather than add a fixed drill block; 10-15 minutes of dedicated play practice daily is a common clinical convention, not a trial-derived threshold.

### Gaps
- No trial-derived answer to "how many minutes per day at home". Trials report clinician contact hours, not home practice minutes, and I found no dose-response analysis for home practice.
- Roberts et al. 2019 dosage moderator results not retrieved.
- No results found for the ABC-PALS trial or any study directly testing which BCTs (video feedback, self-monitoring, goal setting, modelling) causally improve parent uptake in language intervention; the evidence found is descriptive taxonomy work plus programme-owner claims about video feedback.
- LENA Start programme length and the Cunha et al. (NBER 2023, [working paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30837/w30837.pdf)) experimental results not retrieved.
- Number of exposures needed for verbs vs nouns: nothing found.

## Key question 3: What is free to reuse (generic techniques) vs protected (names, manuals)?

### Takeaway
The techniques themselves (following the child's lead, waiting, modelling, expansion, recasting, focused stimulation, time delay, dialogic prompts, parallel/self-talk) are generic clinical methods described in the open research literature and can be taught in original wording; what is protected is branding (programme names, acronyms registered as marks such as Hanen's OWL) and the copyrighted text, illustrations and manuals. Only the Hanen OWL marks were positively verified in this session; other programmes' status below is unverified and needs a trademark-register check plus legal advice.

### Cited Findings
- "OWL" is a registered Canadian trademark of the Hanen Early Language Program (registered 2024-10-17, expiry 2034-10-17) — [CIPO 1881398](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/cipo/trademark-search/1881398)
- "OBSERVE, WAIT AND LISTEN" and "Observe Wait and Listen (OWL)" also appear as Canadian trademark records — [CIPO 1881695](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/cipo/trademark-search/1881695); [CIPO 2203001](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/cipo/trademark-search/2203001) (owner not visible in the snippet for these two)
- Hanen Early Language Program holds US trademark records including "OWL" for publications such as books, teaching guides and instructional outlines — [Justia](https://trademarks.justia.com/owners/hanen-early-language-program-1546379)
- Hanen's own materials mark the strategy as "Observe, Wait and Listen™ (OWL™)" and the programmes as "It Takes Two to Talk®", "Target Word™" — [Hanen handout](https://www.hanen.org/getmedia/acd6de84-76f5-4fde-b885-2e3daf745873/Holiday-Handout-2025.pdf?ext=.pdf); [Hanen Target Word](https://www.hanen.org/programs/for-parents/target-word.aspx)
- Hanen programmes are delivered by Hanen-certified speech-language pathologists/therapists — [UCL review](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/educational-psychology/resources/CS1Bird22-25.pdf)
- In contrast, focused stimulation, modelling of single words, expansions, time delay, milieu prompting and responsive interaction are described in the peer-reviewed literature as generic treatment procedures, not as branded products — [Cable & Domsch 2011](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.3109/13682822.2010.487883); [Kaiser & Hampton EMT chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265037755_Enhanced_Milieu_Teaching)
- "Toy talk" is published as a research strategy by Hadley & Rispoli (University of Illinois) — [Univ. of Illinois](https://www.ahs.illinois.edu/?p=2923)

### Inferences
- Safe to borrow (technique level, in Todug's own Mongolian wording and own examples): follow the child's lead; get face to face; wait silently and expectantly before speaking (Todug's "wait" beat is the generic time-delay / expectant-pause procedure); imitate the child; take balanced turns; label what the child is attending to; repeat a few chosen words many times in varied situations; add one word to what the child said; restate an error correctly without demanding repetition; offer choices; arrange the environment so the child has a reason to communicate; ask open questions during book sharing and expand the answer.
- Avoid: using "OWL", "Observe, Wait and Listen", "It Takes Two to Talk", "Target Word", "More Than Words", "4 S's" as a named framework, or the Discoverer / Communicator / First Words User / Combiner stage labels, as product features or lesson titles; translating or paraphrasing closely from Hanen guidebooks, checklists or illustrations; implying the product is a Hanen, LENA, JASPER, ESDM or ImPACT programme or is endorsed/certified by them. Descriptive citation ("this strategy is similar to what the research literature calls focused stimulation") is ordinary nominative reference, but I am not a lawyer and trademark scope in Mongolia depends on local/Madrid registration, which was not checked.
- A mnemonic of Todug's own in Mongolian for the wait step and the stage names would both avoid the marks and suit the audience better than transliterated English acronyms.
- UNVERIFIED background on other programmes: LENA and LENA Start are marks of the LENA Foundation and depend on proprietary recorder hardware; JASPER, ESDM/P-ESDM and Project ImPACT have published manuals under copyright and formal training/certification routes; "Thirty Million Words" and "3Ts" are branding of the TMW Center (University of Chicago); Vroom is a Bezos Family Foundation brand whose tip content has been offered to partners under licence; Reach Out and Read is a named non-profit model; dialogic reading's PEER and CROWD acronyms come from Whitehurst's academic papers and are widely reproduced in public materials; EMT and Teach-Model-Coach-Review are academic terms from the Kaiser/Roberts groups, with training materials but no consumer brand that I could confirm. All of this needs verification.

### Gaps
- No trademark-register confirmation was obtained for the "4 S's", the four Hanen stage names, "It Takes Two to Talk", "Target Word", "More Than Words", LENA Start, JASPER, ESDM, Project ImPACT, 3Ts, Vroom or Talk With Me Baby; and none in the Mongolian or WIPO Madrid registers.
- No source found on the licensing terms of any free public curricula (e.g. Vroom tips, Talk With Me Baby, Harvard serve-and-return materials) and whether translation/adaptation into Mongolian is permitted.
- Whether Hanen licenses translations or has any Mongolian-language presence was not established.

## Key question 4: What common advice is NOT supported or is contraindicated?

### Takeaway
The clearest evidence-based cautions are: more screen time is associated with poorer language (small effect, observational); telegraphic "baby grammar" input is not supported and may be associated with worse outcomes; brief, low-intensity, information-only parent programmes do not move outcomes; and parent-led articulation work lacks an evidence base without SLP guidance. For "say X" drilling, withholding and flashcards I found no direct trial evidence either way in this session; the case against them rests on the fact that every effective programme is built on responsive, child-led modelling without demanded imitation.

### Cited Findings

**Screens**
- Madigan et al. (2020), *JAMA Pediatrics*, 42 studies: greater quantity of screen use was negatively associated with child language (pooled r = -0.14); better quality (educational programmes, co-viewing with a caregiver) was positively associated; all included studies observational, about 71% television only; authors support limiting duration, choosing quality content and co-viewing — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32202633/); [JAMA Pediatrics](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/2762864)

**Telegraphic input ("want juice", "doggy run" spoken by the adult)**
- van Kleeck et al. (2010) review: very little research; three intervention studies in children with intellectual disability found no comprehension advantage for telegraphic over grammatical input; authors concluded clinical use of telegraphic input cannot be justified from the available research — [PMC7225012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7225012)
- Venker et al. (2015): among parents of preschoolers with ASD, higher rates of parent telegraphic speech were associated with less-developed child language two years later (correlational); the claim that telegraphic models are easier to imitate has "no scientific evidence" behind it — [Venker et al. 2015](https://lingolab.msu.edu/research/publications1/Venker_etal_2015_Parent%20Telegraphic%20Speech.pdf)
- SLPs remain split in practice on telegraphic vs grammatical models — [Vanderbilt CLIMB lab](https://www.vumc.org/climb-lab/publication/speech-language-pathologists-ratings-telegraphic-versus-grammatical-utterances-survey)

**Low-dose, light-touch parent programmes**
- Let's Learn Language: six weekly group sessions for slow-to-talk 18-month-olds produced no language or behaviour benefit at ages 2 or 3 — [BMJ 2011](https://www.doi.org/10.1136/BMJ.D4741)
- P-ESDM at 1 hour/week for 12 weeks: no effect on parent interaction or child outcomes — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23021480/)
- 3Ts pilot: gains in turns and adult words during the programme were not maintained post-intervention — [TMW Center](https://tmwcenter.uchicago.edu/resources/enriching-home-language-environment-among-families-from-low-ses-backgrounds)

**"Wait and see" is not harmless for everyone**
- Late-talker definition (Rescorla): fewer than 50 words on the 310-word Language Development Survey or no two-word combinations at 24 months; about 10-20% of 2-year-olds — [PMC7455001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7455001); [ASHA CICSD](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/cicsd_34_F_118?download=true)
- Most late talkers score in the average range by age 5-7, but more than half were still delayed in syntax at 3 and 29% at 4, and late talking raised risk of low vocabulary at 48 months and low school readiness at 60 months — [Rescorla 2011](https://talkbank.org/childes/access/Clinical-Eng/0docs/rescorla2011.pdf); [PMC2862655](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2862655)
- Diagnosis before 24 months is unreliable because of high variability in early vocabulary growth — [PMC5962923](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962923)
- In the HPLI RCT, 26.1% of untreated waiting-list late talkers met criteria for specific language impairment a year later vs 8.3% of treated — [PMC2614563](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2614563/)

**Articulation / speech-sound work at home**
- Sugden et al. (2016), *IJLCD* 51(6):597-625: of 176 speech-sound-disorder intervention papers, 61 mentioned parent involvement or home tasks, but most gave limited detail on what parents did; more and better research needed — [ETSU record](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works/1996)
- Pritchard et al. (2024) scoping review: clear information from the SLT supports fidelity of and engagement with home practice (i.e. home practice is studied as SLT-directed, not parent-initiated) — [PMC11606383](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11606383/)
- A realist-review protocol (2023) states that what makes parent-implemented SSD intervention effective, for whom and when, is still unknown — [PMC10773357](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10773357/)

**Clinic-based book giveaways as a language intervention**
- Reach Out and Read: rated "Scientifically Supported" for increasing how often low-income parents read to children — [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/strategies-and-solutions/what-works-for-health/strategies/reach-out-and-read); but the UK Foundations guidebook rates the child-outcome evidence only as preliminary ("cannot be confident the intervention caused the improvement"), resting on a single US comparison-group study — [Foundations](https://foundations.org.uk/guidebook/reach-out-and-read)

### Inferences
- "Say X" / drilling / withholding: none of the effective programmes found (focused stimulation, HPLI, EMT's responsive core, toy talk, dialogic reading) depend on demanded imitation, and focused stimulation is defined by modelling without requiring a response. EMT does include prompts (time delay, mand-model, "say" prompts within milieu prompting), but as the last and least-used layer, only for things the child is already requesting, inside a responsive interaction. So the defensible product rule is: model and wait; never refuse the item until the child says the word; if a prompt is used, use one, then give the item and the model regardless. This is an inference from programme design; I found no head-to-head trial of "say X" drilling vs modelling in late talkers.
- Todug's scripted dialogues should have the parent speak short but grammatical sentences (Mongolian case/verb endings intact) rather than stripped two-word "telegraphic" models, and should avoid instructions like "make the child repeat".
- Flashcards: no evidence found for or against in this session. The Heidlage finding that play/routine-based delivery (g = 0.50) performs at least as well as book reading supports teaching words on real objects and actions in routines, and verbs in particular cannot be modelled well on static cards. Treat "flashcards are not supported" as an absence-of-evidence statement, not a proven harm.
- Screens: the evidence is correlational and small (r = -0.14); the accurate message is "screens displace conversation; co-view and talk about it", not "screens cause delay". For Todug itself this argues for parent-facing content that sends the parent off-screen to interact, not child-facing videos.
- Articulation (stage 11 of the ladder): the literature only supports parent speech-sound practice as homework set by an SLP after assessment. Without assessment a parent cannot tell developmental errors from disorder, or pick targets. Reasonable product position: for children under about 3-4, or with few words, do no sound drills at all, only recast clearly and respond to meaning; restrict the articulation stage to general listening/sound-play and clear modelling, and route persistent unintelligibility, sound loss, suspected hearing problems, feeding/structural concerns, stuttering or regression to a professional. The specific age thresholds here are my judgement, not sourced.
- A non-diagnostic product should still carry explicit referral prompts: no words by about 18 months, fewer than 50 words or no combinations at 24 months, loss of skills, poor comprehension, limited gesture/joint attention, or hearing concerns. Only the 24-month criterion is sourced above (Rescorla); the others are standard red flags from background knowledge and should be verified against a paediatric guideline.

### Gaps
- No direct study retrieved on elicited-imitation / "say X" drilling, on withholding desired items, or on flashcards in late talkers.
- No source retrieved on negative effects of parental pressure or over-questioning (test questions) on late talkers, though the Girolametto/Hanen literature on parental directiveness is the place to look.
- No sourced age norms for speech-sound acquisition or sourced referral criteria for speech sound disorder were retrieved.
- Bilingualism advice (e.g. "drop the second language"), pacifier and oral-motor exercise claims were not searched; non-speech oral-motor exercises in particular are a commonly cited unsupported practice worth a dedicated check before the articulation stage is written.
