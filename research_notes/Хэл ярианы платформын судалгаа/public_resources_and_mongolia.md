## Which resources can be legally translated/adapted into Mongolian and under what conditions?

### Takeaway
Almost none of the well-known parent resources are openly licensed: only the WHO/UNICEF Nurturing Care family (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO) and, with an email request and attribution, the CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early." materials were confirmed as translatable without a negotiated licence; everything else (Raising Children, Zero to Three, Harvard, Hanen, ASHA, First Words Project, Vroom, UNICEF Parenting, BBC) is all-rights-reserved or permission/licence-based. The safe pattern for Todug is to translate the two open sources with the required attribution/disclaimers, and treat the rest as *ideas and evidence to cite and re-express in original Mongolian wording*, not as text to translate.

### Inferences
- **Translate directly (with conditions):** (a) WHO/UNICEF Nurturing Care materials, provided Todug's derived text is non-commercial and itself released under CC BY-NC-SA, with the translation disclaimer and no WHO/UNICEF logos; (b) CDC LTSAE milestone checklists and tips after emailing ActEarly@cdc.gov, with the CDC credit line. The NC and SA clauses are a real constraint if Todug charges for the plan: a paid product built on CC BY-NC-SA text is likely non-compliant, so keep any such translated material in a free, separately licensed section, or get legal advice.
- **Milestones are facts, wording is not.** Ages at which skills appear (e.g., "points by 12 months") are not copyrightable facts, but the selection, wording and layout of a checklist are. Todug can state milestones in its own Mongolian wording and cite sources without a licence; it cannot translate a Hanen tip sheet, a Raising Children article or the "16 Gestures" graphic.
- **Strategy names are trademarks.** "Serve and return" as a concept is freely describable, but Hanen programme names ("It Takes Two to Talk", "OWL"), "Vroom", "Talking is Teaching" and "FIRST WORDS" are marks; avoid using them as feature names.
- **CDC milestones are US/English norms.** Translating the CDC language items literally would repeat the exact problem Todug wants to remove (English-based word-combination milestones); use CDC for social-communication, gesture and play items and write Mongolian-specific language items separately.
- A Mongolian CDI would be a research project (authorization + norming), not a content task; a lighter option is an unnormed, clearly labelled Mongolian "word and suffix checklist" that does not use the CDI name.

## What Mongolian-specific developmental facts should restructure the stage ladder and articulation content?

### Takeaway
There is essentially no published acquisition research on Mongolian grammar (no study of first words, case suffixes, verb morphology or MLU was found), so any morphology-based restructuring must be labelled as inference from Turkish and Korean, where children use productive suffixes at the one-word stage, well before or alongside word combinations. For articulation there is one large Mongolian dataset (1,083 children aged 2-5, MNUE, 2022) confirming front-to-back emergence and identifying р, с, ц, ш, з, ж, ч as the hardest sounds, consistent with local teaching materials that place р and л last (5-6 years).

### Cited Findings

**Mongolian-language evidence (direct)**
- A study at the Mongolian National University of Education tested 1,083 randomly selected Mongolian-speaking children aged 2-5 with a single-sound and single-word naming task. — [Batkhuyag, QAHE 2022, Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/qahe-22/125979735)
- It found that children first pronounce clearly the sounds made at the front of the mouth; sounds made in the middle and back become clear later. — [Batkhuyag 2022](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/qahe-22/125979735)
- It lists the most challenging sounds for young children as [r], [s], [ts], [sh], [dz], [dj] and [ch]. — [Batkhuyag 2022 PDF](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125979735.pdf)
- The abstract notes that Mongolian sounds differ from other languages in requiring flexibility and muscle development of the speech organs. — [Batkhuyag 2022](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/qahe-22/125979735)
- A Mongolian kindergarten teaching source gives this age schedule: 1-2 years а, о, э, п, б, м; 2-3 years и, ы, у, т, д, н, г, к, х, в; 3-5 years с, з, ц, ш, ж, ч; 5-6 years р, л (practitioner material, no methodology stated). — [Orkhon aimag kindergarten no. 19](http://garden-19.blogspot.com/2018/03/blog-post_93.html)
- A published Mongolian book series for developing correct pronunciation ("Авиан зөв дуудлагыг хөгжүүлэх цуврал", Jangar) exists in five age-graded volumes, made with a preschool specialist. — [ikon.mn](https://ikon.mn/n/1e66)
- Phonetics: Khalkha Mongolian has two liquids, /ɮ/ (a lateral fricative, often devoiced to [ɬ]) and /r/ (usually a dental trill, sometimes fricative, especially word-finally). — [Ueta, Hokkaido University](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/84903/08_Ueta.pdf)
- Grammar facts: Mongolian is agglutinative, building words by suffixes; nouns take case, possession and number; verbs mark aspect, tense and mood but not person or number. — [Languages Gulper: Mongolian](https://languagesgulper.com/eng/Mongolian.html)
- Traditional grammar describes eight cases; one comparative analysis counts 12 in active use across Mongolic varieties. — [Mongolic case suffixes article](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/modi-2024-0007/html?lang=en)
- A comparative observation study of 3-4-year-old Mongolian and Korean children's speech and cognition exists (conversation analysis on set tasks); details not accessible. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388117437_HHDIJN_HEL_ARIANY_HGZIL_BA_TANIN_MEDEHJN_ONCLOGT_HIJSEN_AZIGLALT_3-4_nastaj_Mongol_Solongos_hhdijn_ziseen_deer)
- MNUE's journal Lavai published a review of research on Mongolian child development (2009). — [Lavai vol. 4](https://lavai.msue.edu.mn/ppapers/04-LAV-005.pdf)

**Typologically similar languages (indirect)**
- Turkish: early utterances already contain stressed suffixes in the correct agglutinative order; productive case inflection on nouns is reported at around 15 months, i.e., productive morphology at the one-word stage. — [The Acquisition of Turkish (Aksu-Koç & Slobin)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346833292_The_Acquisition_of_Turkish)
- Turkish: the first verbal inflection is the direct-experience past -DI, emerging between 1;6 and 2;0; voice and modality inflections are mastered by two to three years. — [Early verbal morphology in Turkish](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110899832.27)
- Turkish: many inflections emerge early but case and agreement continue to be refined into the school years. — [Acquisition of modality in Turkish](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368112024_Acquisition_of_modality_in_Turkish)
- Korean: from the one-word stage children use sentence-ending suffixes appropriately in discourse; by age four they acquire (or begin to acquire) seven such suffixes in a specific order (five children, 1;8-4;0). — [Choi, sentence-ending markers](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781501504457-014/html)
- Korean: comprehension lags production; children cannot use case markers to interpret OSV sentences until after age three and a half. — [BUCLD 43](https://www.lingref.com/bucld/43/BUCLD43-49.pdf)
- Korean: noun ellipsis makes verbs more prominent in input (higher verb type and token frequency); Korean learners showed early verb productivity and learned more verbs than English learners. — [Systematic review of noun bias, PMC13347766](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13347766/); [Choi 2000, J. Child Lang.](https://schoi.sdsu.edu/docs/choi_input_JCL_2000.pdf)
- The evidence is contested: other studies of "verb-friendly" languages still find more nouns than verbs (Au et al. 1994 and Kim et al. 2000 for Korean; Ogura 2001 for Japanese), and a German-Korean-Turkish comparison found a noun advantage in all three, differing only in size. — [Systematic review, PMC13347766](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13347766/); [German/Korean/Turkish study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/247514728_Similarities_and_variation_in_noun_and_verb_acquisition_A_crosslinguistic_study_of_children_learning_German_Korean_and_Turkish)
- Japanese children were reported to learn verbs more rapidly than English-speaking children in one comparison. — [Japanese/English caregiver input study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/referential-transparency-of-verbs-in-childdirected-input-by-japanese-and-american-caregivers/913BEF44B8E2843630431C555121789F)
- Cross-linguistically, the apical trill /r/ is late-acquired and replaced by gliding, stopping, lateralization or fricatives (Setswana data). — [PMC5280064](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5280064)
- McLeod & Crowe's review of consonant acquisition in 27 languages is the standard cross-linguistic reference for acquisition ages. — [AJSLP review](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2018_ajslp-17-0100)

### Inferences
All of the following are **inferences from Turkish/Korean/Japanese plus Mongolian grammar, not from Mongolian child data**.
- **Replace "2-word combination" with "word + suffix OR two words" as the first grammar milestone.** In a suffixing SOV language with free argument drop, a single inflected word (аав-ын "daddy's", гэр-т "at home", ид-нэ "will eat", ав-аад "take and...", яв-ъя "let's go") is a complete, adult-like utterance. A Mongolian child may show grammatical progress through suffixes while still producing one "word" at a time. A ladder that waits for two separate words will under-credit typical children and mistarget practice.
- **Count morphemes, not words.** MLU in words systematically understates level in agglutinative languages; if Todug tracks progress, count meaningful parts (stem + each suffix). No Mongolian MLU norms exist, so use it only for within-child progress, never as a pass/fail norm.
- **Put verbs and verb endings early in vocabulary targets.** Mongolian is verb-final and drops subjects/objects, so (as argued for Korean) verbs are likely frequent and salient in parental speech. Early word lists should mix action words and imperative/hortative forms (ав, өг, ир, яв, суу; явъя, идье) with nouns, rather than the noun-heavy "first 50 words" lists typical of English material. The noun-bias literature is disputed, so present this as "include verbs early", not "verbs come first".
- **Likely early suffix candidates** (by analogy with Turkish past -DI and Korean sentence enders, plus frequency in everyday Mongolian speech): imperative bare stem, past -сан/-лаа, non-past -на, hortative -я/-ё, genitive -ын/-ийн (possession), dative-locative -д/-т, accusative -ыг/-ийг, comitative -тай, negation -гүй, question particles уу/үү, бэ/вэ. Order is unknown for Mongolian; a suggested ladder is: single bare words → inflected single words (verb endings, possession, -гүй) → two-part utterances (noun + verb, noun-case + verb) → three-part SOV sentences with case → converbs and clause chaining (-аад, -ж) for narrative → questions with particles and wh-words.
- **Narrative stage should target converb chaining**, since Mongolian links events with converbs (-аад, -ж, -хад) rather than "and then"; Korean clause-chaining research suggests this is a distinct later development.
- **Vowel harmony** is probably acquired early and largely error-free (as reported for Turkish), so it need not be a taught stage; parents should simply model natural forms. This is unverified for Mongolian.
- **Articulation ladder:** order practice front-to-back and treat р, л, с, ш, з, ж, ц, ч as expected-late sounds; do not flag them before roughly 4-5 years (р/л up to 5-6) based on local practitioner schedules. Because Mongolian "л" is a lateral fricative [ɮ]/[ɬ], not the English/Russian [l], English or Russian articulation exercises for /l/ are not valid for Mongolian; likewise "х" is a back fricative and affricates ц/ч/з/ж have no direct counterparts in English charts. Articulation content must be written for Mongolian, not translated.
- **Comprehension of case-marked non-canonical order comes late** (Korean: after 3;6), so parents should use canonical SOV order with clear case suffixes in early stages.

A MNUE dissertation or Lavai article may exist offline; the 2009 Lavai review and the 3-4-year-old Mongolian/Korean study could not be opened.

## What local competitors and gaps exist?

### Takeaway
Mongolia has very few speech therapists, no established degree route for them, and no speech-language screening in routine child checks; the government only announced in November 2026 that it will study children's speech development, plan the workforce and add speech assessment to preventive check-ups. No Mongolian-language digital product for parents of late-talking toddlers was found: existing offerings are in-person clinics, a printed pronunciation book series, and general kids' learning apps for ages 3+.

### Cited Findings

**Policy, screening tools and data**
- In November 2026 the Ministry of Health and the National Council for Language Policy agreed to cooperate on children's speech development: study the current state, estimate the need for speech-therapy professionals and plan human resources, train speech therapists, introduce speech-language assessment into preventive child check-ups and early detection, develop recommendations for parents and caregivers, and improve service standards. — [Montsame](https://montsame.mn/mn/article/5-J9Q1TLqs46Fzd536tFP)
- The same period saw a Ministry of Education plan for a national programme on mother-tongue education and reading skills. — [Montsame](https://montsame.mn/mn/article/FoYjTefXFxC4X1Z4YjWG-)
- A state official (E. Batshugar) stated that children's speech development will be supported from an early age. — [MNB](https://www.mnb.mn/article/e-batshugar-khuukhdiin-khel-yariany-khugjliig-baga-nasnaas-n-demjine-5856)
- Mongolia has a locally developed screening tool, the Mongolian Rapid Baby Scale (MORBAS): 161 items in seven domains including separate expressive and receptive language domains, ages 0-42 months, about 15 minutes; validated against Bayley-III in 150 Ulaanbaatar children with correlations of 0.86-0.97, sensitivity 81.8%, specificity 52.3%. — [Dagvadorj et al., Acta Paediatrica 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25443770/)
- The MORBAS authors described the lack of a convenient screening tool as a significant barrier in Mongolia. — [Acta Paediatrica 2015](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/apa.12884)
- MORBAS was later used to assess developmental delay and predictors in Mongolian children aged 13-24 months. — [BMC Pediatrics](https://bmcpediatr.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12887-018-1017-y)
- Family health centres use the Maternal and Child Health handbook for young-child checks but do not run scheduled universal check-ups as in Japan; JICA piloted a comprehensive 18-month health and development check in Bayangol district from May 2017, and in Khuvsgul aimag. — [JICA project news](https://www.jica.go.jp/project/mongolian/mongolia/013/news/general/170904.html)
- A Ministry of Education guide on using the MCH handbook exists, and officials say the handbook helps detect disability early. — [moe.gov.mn](https://moe.gov.mn/post/123175); [Montsame](https://montsame.mn/mn/read/200965)
- UNICEF data page for Mongolia lists 34% of under-fives with three or more children's books and 69% playing with two or more types of playthings. — [UNICEF data: Mongolia](https://data.unicef.org/country/mng/)
- Father engagement in early stimulation in Mongolia was 40-49% across MICS rounds 2000-2013. — [J Child Fam Stud 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-021-02006-1)
- An evidence brief notes Mongolian parents reported limited involvement in early-learning activities at home. — [HKU Mongolia Evidence Brief](https://hkece.edu.hku.hk/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Mongolia-Evidence-Brief.pdf)

**Speech-therapy workforce and services**

- A kindergarten director (Dr J. ### Inferences
- **The gap is real and currently unfilled:** no Mongolian parent-coaching product for late talkers aged 1-3 appeared in any search; competitors are clinics (in-person, UB-centred), books, and 3+ learning apps focused on letters, numbers and English. Todug's 1-5 home-practice plan is not duplicated by anything found.
- **Timing is favourable:** the November 2026 MoH/Language Policy Council agreement explicitly calls for parent recommendations and speech assessment in check-ups. Todug could align its stage ladder and red-flag wording with whatever that process produces, and position itself as a complement to (not substitute for) the scarce therapists.
- **Existing national programmes target school readiness at age 5**, not early language at 1-3; the youngest age band is the least served.
- **Use MORBAS and the MCH handbook as local anchors** for milestone wording and referral advice rather than ASQ/CDC cut-offs, since MORBAS is the only locally validated tool with separate language domains.
- **Screen/foreign-language messaging should be careful:** local experts link phones and early foreign-language exposure to speech problems, but the evidence is correlational and the popular "4% to 25%" statistic is unsourced. Recommended framing: the risk is displacement of live Mongolian interaction, not bilingualism itself; do not repeat the 25% figure as fact.
- Urban/rural gap: therapists and centres found are all in Ulaanbaatar and special schools; a web platform is one of few options for aimag and herder families, but content must work with low connectivity and grandparents/relatives as caregivers.

### Gaps
- **No figures found** for: number of speech therapists in Mongolia, session prices, waiting times, the size of MNUE's special-needs/defectology intake, or measured prevalence of speech delay. Experts themselves say statistics do not exist; the government study announced in November 2026 has not reported.
- The Montsame, Unuudur and MNB articles could not be opened, so details (dates, exact quotes, any numbers) of the November 2026 agreement are from search extracts only.
- No Mongolian study or official guidance was found on children acquiring English/Russian/Chinese from YouTube instead of Mongolian; the concern appears in expert interviews and is widely discussed anecdotally, but I found no data. No national screen-time guideline for Mongolia was found.
- No evidence found of a Mongolian ASQ-3 validation or routine Bayley/Denver use beyond Bayley-III serving as the criterion in the MORBAS validation; the language items of the MCH handbook were not seen.
- Facebook groups, Instagram/TikTok speech-therapy accounts and paid online courses for parents are likely the main informal competitors but are not indexed by web search; a manual scan of Facebook (search "хэл засал", "хэл засалч", "хэлд оруулах") is needed.
- No World Vision Mongolia early-language programme was found; JICA and Save the Children work found is disability/early-detection and school-readiness focused, not parent language coaching.
